Leadership and management: how you actually lead

Operating rhythm, incident command, a struggling lead, and a frame for live scenarios

NOT: Leadership as a personality trait or a list of buzzwords like "servant leader".
IS: A repeatable system: a rhythm with your leads, clear roles in a crisis, fair and early feedback, and a calm way to think aloud.

  1. How you lead through team leads: a rhythm, not a personality.🛺 Head chef and sous-chefs: standards from the top, each station run by its owner.
  2. Set outcomes and guardrails with each lead. They own the how.
  3. Weekly one-to-one with each lead (coaching, decisions, early bad news) and a short weekly delivery and risk review.
  4. Monthly skip-levels with engineers: hear what leads may not tell you, check morale and on-call load.
  5. Quarterly roadmap and capability review: priorities, succession, skills gaps.
  6. A shared dashboard (delivery, quality, incidents, people) means I stay informed without micromanaging. I step in only when it turns red.
  1. A production incident, step by step. Use this shape for every incident story.🛺 Fire drill: one warden in charge, clear roles, get people out first, investigate after.
  2. Detect: an alert or a customer reports it.
  3. Declare: set the severity and name one incident commander.
  4. Roles: comms, scribe, and the right experts only. Avoid a crowd on the call.
  5. Mitigate first: rollback or failover before deep diagnosis.
  6. Communicate: regular updates to stakeholders, even if the update is "no change".
  7. Recover: confirm with metrics, not with feelings.
  8. Blameless review within days: what in the system allowed this?
  9. Actions land: owners and dates, and you check they are done. This is what panels remember.
  1. A team lead is not delivering. The panel tests courage and fairness together.🛺 Like a coach with a player who is out of form: honest feedback, a training plan, and a fair selection decision.
  2. Facts first: look at outcomes and examples, not impressions.
  3. Feedback privately and specifically, soon, not at the annual review.
  4. Agree a 30 to 60 day plan with clear goals and real support: coaching, reduced scope.
  5. Check weekly and keep short written notes.
  6. If they improve, recognise it publicly.
  7. If not, decide fairly: a role change or an exit, with dignity.
  8. Throughout, protect the team: a senior engineer covers gaps so delivery does not suffer.
  1. Five steps for any "what would you do if…" question. About 60 seconds.🛺 Like a doctor in casualty: ask, triage, treat, tell the family, then fix the process.
  2. Clarify one or two facts out loud. It shows judgement and buys thinking time.
  3. Prioritise: people and risk first, schedule after.
  4. Act in three clear steps, in the order you would do them.
  5. Communicate: name who needs to hear what, and when.
  6. Learn: say how you prevent a repeat. That is the senior part.
  7. Try it on a real prompt, out loud, with a timer.
